### EDA-P-03: An event is an immutable fact about something that has happened

#### Description

The meaning of an event becomes even clearer when we compare it with _command_ and _document_ messages. Based on Gregor Hohpe's _Enterprise Integration Patterns_, these three message types serve fundamentally different purposes.

Core distinctions:

*	An _event_ is an immutable fact representing something that has already happened. When a system publishes 'OrderPlaced', it is announcing a completed fact. Time frame: past tense
*	A _command_ is an instruction requesting that something should happen in the future. 'PlaceOrder' expresses an intention can be accepted, rejected, or fail. Time frame: future tense
*	A _document_ simply transfers data without behavioral intent. An 'Order' document contains information but but does not by itself indicate whether something has happened or should happen. Time frame: present tense.

Note that Hohpe’s terminology is followed here. Alternative terms for document include: _query_, _state_, or _message_. These are less descriptive in this context: _query_ is usually a specialization of a command, _state_ is too general, and _message_ is the generic container term.

In contrast to a command, an event carries no intention and has no recipient. Events usually follow a publish/subscribe pattern (one-to-many), where a single event may be consumed by multiple independent subscribers. Commands, on the other hand, typically follow a point-to-point pattern (one-to-one), since they are directed at a specific handler. Documents also tend to use point-to-point communication. While they could be shared with multiple recipients, in practice they are most often exchanged directly between known parties - especially in inter-organizational or business-to-business scenarios. [Richards 2021].

#### Rationale

*	Stable and reliable data: Events represent immutable facts about what happened in the past - they cannot be altered or deleted after creation. This stability creates a trustworthy foundation for all downstream systems, compliance requirements, and business decisions.
*	Architectural resilience: Modern distributed systems require loose coupling to achieve scalability, fault tolerance, and independent evolution of components. Point-to-point communication creates tight dependencies that make systems brittle and difficult to change. Events as immutable facts enable systems to communicate what happened without requiring knowledge of who needs to know, creating natural decoupling.
*	Operational intelligence: Immutable events preserve the full timeline of business activities, enabling analytics, machine learning, and business intelligence that would be difficult with mutable records.
*	Future flexibility: Business requirements evolve, but historical data remains valuable. Immutable events allow new systems and processes to be built on top of existing event streams without disrupting current operations, supporting organizational agility and system evolution.
*	Allow for choreography: By publishing immutable business events, systems enable choreographed workflows where multiple services can independently react to the same business fact, creating emergent business processes without central control.

#### Implication

*	Producer/consumer decoupling: Since events are not directed to one or more specific recipients, the messaging style of data exchange between known actors (point-to-point) does not apply here. In the event-driven style, the main task of a producer is to publish events. These events can then be delivered in various ways, e.g. via an intermediary, to zero, one, or multiple consumers. Unlike in point-to-point communication, consumers do not necessarily need to know the producer. Producers and consumers are therefore technically fully decoupled.
*	Event naming: Events must be named to reflect completed facts (past tense).
*	Events as first-class, authoritative records: In essence, treating events as immutable facts is what gives them the reliability and permanence necessary to serve as first-class architectural elements. If events could be changed after creation, they would lose much of their value as authoritative sources of truth and wouldn't merit first-class treatment in system design.
*	State reconstruction: Because events are immutable facts, you can replay them in sequence to reconstruct any historical system state. This is only possible because each event represents a fixed point in time that never changes.
*	Schema evolution: Since events are immutable, evolution must be backward/forward compatible and should include version metadata to support evolution.
*	Security, privacy and compliance: Events can contain sensitive data; policies for PII, encryption, and redaction must be applied, and legal/retention requirements (e.g.: GDPR) must be considered when storing immutable records.
