### EDA-P-12: Event-driven systems are governed

| Strategy | Event-driven systems are governed. |

#### Description

Event-driven architectures require structured governance to ensure consistency, reliability, and maintainability within and across enterprises. This includes establishing clear ownership of events and services, implementing schema management processes with proper versioning and change control, and defining standards for event design and evolution.

Governance also includes event discoverability, ensuring that available events, their schemas, and their purposes are easily findable and understood by development teams across the organization and external partners.

Cross-enterprise governance addresses event sharing protocols, security standards, data privacy compliance, and interoperability requirements when events flow between different organizations.

Governance ensures that events remain aligned with business domains, breaking changes are managed appropriately, and the overall system architecture maintains integrity as it scales both internally and across organizational boundaries.

Organizationally, governance necessitates cross-functional coordination (architecture board, domain stewards, platform teams) and documented change-control processes to manage versioning and cross-enterprise sharing.

Without proper governance, event-driven systems risk becoming fragmented, with incompatible schemas, unclear ownership, hidden or undiscoverable events, and uncontrolled evolution that undermines the architecture's benefits.

Governance in event-driven architecture is a shared responsibility - producers, consumers, and support teams must collaborate to achieve it.

#### Rationale

*	Maintain event quality: Governance ensures that events carry the right metadata, follow naming conventions, and adhere to payload standards - maintaining event quality across systems.
*	Secure by design: Unlike RESTful APIs (with straightforward security per endpoint), event systems operate via publish/subscribe channels featuring producers, consumers, various protocols, and distributed brokers. Governance enables consistent enforcement of security, access control, authentication, and authorization across this complex landscape.
*	Enhance discoverability: Governance introduces event catalogs or portals - central registries of documented, versioned, and discoverable events. This drives reusability, accelerates development, and prevents duplication.
*	Enable traceability: Governance ensures that events can be tracked, audited, and versioned, helping with debugging, compliance, and change management. Event portals often support lifecycle management, tracking changes from design to deployment.
*	Protocols and tools: Event-driven architectures leverage different protocols (Kafka, MQTT, AMQP) and standards (CloudEvents, AsyncAPI). Governance must account for protocol-specific features and limitations - ensuring that policies, security rules, and naming conventions are consistently applied regardless of underlying platform.
*	Organizational governance: Team responsibilities, ownership models, and development processes.

#### Implication

*	Ownership and roles: Every event/topic needs a designated owner and clear role definitions (Event Owner, Platform Team, Governance Board) responsible for approvals, incident response, and documentation.
*	Event catalog: A central registry or portal must exist for event discoverability, metadata, and enforced schema publication to prevent duplication and enable automated validation.
*	Versioning & change control: Define lifecycle states (draft → published → deprecated → retired), compatible/incompatible change rules, and deprecation timelines so consumers can depend on stable contracts.
*	Observability & auditing: Mandate tracing, logging, metrics, and audit trails (correlation IDs, retention rules) so events are traceable for debugging, forensic analysis, and compliance.
*	Operational SLAs & resilience: Publish SLAs, runbooks, and escalation paths for producers and platform services; agree on retry/compensation strategies for transient failures.
*	Security, access control & compliance: Apply uniform policies for authentication, authorization, encryption, and data classification across producers, brokers, and consumers to reduce the scope of impact and meet regulatory needs.
*	Cross enterprise interoperability: Additional agreements and onboarding processes are required for sharing events across business units or external partners, covering semantics, security, and legal terms.
