### EDA-P-01: Event-Driven Architecture is driven by the business domain

#### Description

In Event-Driven Architecture (EDA), events are captured, processed, and distributed to provide real-time insight into business activities and to drive business processes and decision-making. The business domain drives the architecture forward, ensuring the technical implementation remains a faithful reflection of business reality rather than a technical abstraction that happens to use events for communication.

EDA aligns naturally with Domain-Driven Design (DDD), as both share a fundamental principle: being driven by the business domain and its language. Events are prominent in DDD - domain events capture business facts, and when needed, serve as the system's source of truth (for example, via event sourcing). The architecture 'speaks' the language of the business domain, making EDA particularly suitable for communication between Bounded Contexts.

This business focus distinguishes event-driven systems from event-based systems. While event-driven systems are fundamentally shaped by the business meaning of events, event-based systems use events as a technical mechanism. Event-based systems react to low-level signals or triggers - system logs, cache invalidations, or infrastructure events - where events serve merely as the technical foundation rather than as business forces that shape system design, behavior, and evolution.

The distinction is clear: event-driven focuses on events related to business activities and processes, while event-based focuses on events related to technical infrastructure and systems. Event-driven architecture transforms events from technical signals into meaningful business concepts that drive both system behavior and organizational understanding. Although technical events remain important in event-driven systems, they serve as supporting details rather than the primary architectural drivers.

#### Rationale

*	<u>Strategic business alignment</u>: EDA minimizes the translation layer between business concepts and technical implementations by making business events the primary architectural driver. When events represent real domain activities (e.g., 'OrderPlaced', 'MeterConnected', 'CustomerSubscriptionExpired'), the system architecture becomes a close reflection of business processes.
*	<u>Shared language</u>: Driven by the business domain, the business domain language becomes the event-driven system's vocabulary, creating shared understanding between developers, business analysts, and domain experts. EDA allows your business story to be expressed through its technical implementation.
*	<u>Domain-Driven Design integration</u>: EDA's business-domain focus creates natural alignment with DDD principles. Both approaches share the fundamental principle of being driven by the business domain and its language. This alignment makes DDD patterns - domain events, event sourcing, and bounded context communication - directly applicable in event-driven systems, where business-meaningful events (rather than technical signals) drive architectural decisions and system behavior.

#### Implication

*	<u>NBility framework implementation</u>: Business-driven EDA enables implementation of the NBility Model's shared capability framework. Events representing NBility business capabilities create traceable connections between the framework's common language and technical systems, resulting in responsive, loosely coupled services that align with standardized energy sector business processes.
*	<u>Event definition</u>: Events must be defined as business facts that encode real organizational activities, using domain language that business stakeholders understand.
*	<u>Architecture evolution</u>: Systems must evolve based on business domain changes rather than technical convenience.
*	<u>Standardization</u>: The NBility model provides a standardized business capability structure that reinforces business-driven design. Having a shared capability framework helps teams consistently model events around business concepts rather than drifting toward purely technical event-based patterns, creating sector-wide consistency in how business capabilities are expressed through events.
*	<u>Cross-organizational consistency</u>: Standardized business event taxonomies enable better collaboration across industry participants.
*	<u>Ownership</u>: Business domains are the logical owners of events - they decide what counts as an event, its semantics, naming, and when it should fire based on business rules and impact (e.g.: 'OrderPlaced'). Technical teams own the implementation - schemas, validation, delivery, infrastructure and reliability - translating business intent into robust, testable behaviour. This separation ensures events stay domain-driven and valuable while being correctly and reliably executed.
